  ## Executive Summary

  Phase 1 of the XShield project reported near-perfect benchmark performance across both primary evaluation datasets:
  - **TQH-C2 (Testbed A - Sliver / TLS)**: 100.00% Accuracy, 1.0000 Macro F1, 1.0000 ROC-AUC, 0.00% False Positive Rate.
  - **CICIDS-2018 (Botnet)**: 99.99% Accuracy, 0.9998 Macro F1, 1.0000 ROC-AUC.

  The objective of this Phase 3 audit was to investigate why Phase 1 reports near-perfect performance, whether the results are supported by a valid evaluation protocol, whether dataset shortcuts are present, and how well performance holds on unseen capture groups and across testbeds.

  ### Key Findings & Verdict

  1. **Deterministic Shortcuts in TQH-C2**:
    - **Destination Port (`resp_p`)**: 100% of malicious C2 flows (12,038 / 12,038) target port `8443`. Zero benign flows target port `8443` (benign flows target ports `443`, `0`, and `123`). A single-feature model using only `resp_p` achieves **100.00% Balanced Accuracy and 0.00% FPR**.
    - **TLS Fingerprint (`ja4`)**: 99.94% of C2 flows (12,031 / 12,038) exhibit the identical client fingerprint `t13i131000_f57a46bbacb6_e5728521abd4`. This string appears in **0 benign flows**. A single-feature `ja4` model achieves **99.97% Balanced Accuracy**.
    - **Connection State (`conn_state`)**: 94.26% of C2 connections terminate in `RSTO` (TCP reset by originator), while 85.21% of benign flows terminate normally in `SF`. A single-feature `conn_state` model achieves **97.14% Balanced Accuracy**.
    - **Destination IP (`resp_h`)**: 100% of C2 flows connect to a single internal testbed IP (`172.20.0.10`), while benign flows connect to 113 external addresses with zero overlap.

  2. **Group Separation Integrity in TQH-C2**:
    - The 12 capture runs in Testbed A were audited across a 4-fold `GroupKFold` split partitioned strictly by `capture_id`. We verified that **zero capture groups overlap between training and test sets**.
    - However, even when evaluated strictly on unseen capture groups and with ports and TLS fingerprints removed, within-testbed performance remains near 100%. This is because all Testbed A malicious flows were generated by the same automated Sliver C2 beaconing script inside the same synthetic container, creating an artificial uniformity in packet sizes and flow duration that does not reflect real-world beaconing variance.

  3. **Catastrophic Cross-Testbed Generalization Collapse**:
    - When the pure flow-statistics model trained on Testbed A (Sliver / TLS) was evaluated out-of-distribution without retraining on **Testbed B (Merlin / QUIC)**, Balanced Accuracy collapsed to **55.08%** (Macro F1 = 54.71%), and the False Negative Rate rose to **88.27%** (286 of 324 C2 flows were missed entirely; only 38 detected).
    - When evaluated on **Testbed C (Mythic / HTTP+AES)**, performance collapsed completely: Accuracy dropped to **38.52%**, Balanced Accuracy fell to **44.62%** (worse than random guessing), the False Negative Rate reached **94.87%** (12,147 of 12,804 C2 flows missed), and the False Positive Rate jumped to **15.89%** (1,490 false alarms).
    - **Conclusion**: The model does not generalize across C2 frameworks or network transport protocols. It learned the exact flow profile of a specific automated Sliver client in a specific virtual sandbox.

  4. **CICIDS-2018 Duplication, Port Shortcut, and Split Contamination**:
    - **Massive Record Duplication**: 38,822 of 99,538 rows (**39.00%**) in the dataset are exact duplicate feature vectors. 38,690 of these duplicates reside in the `Bot` class.
    - **Random Split Contamination**: In a standard stratified 80/20 random split, **39.48% (7,859 of 19,908 test flows)** have identical twins in the training set. The test evaluation was largely evaluating memorization of training records.
    - **Port Shortcut (`Dst Port`)**: 98.59% of Bot flows (78,597 / 79,720) connect to port `8080`. Exactly 0 benign flows connect to port 8080. A single-feature `Dst Port` classifier achieves **99.84% Accuracy and 0.9996 ROC-AUC**.
    - **Temporal Bursting**: All bot flows were captured during two narrow time windows (01:00-03:00 and 10:00-12:00); no bot flows exist during intermediate hours.

  ### 2.2 Dataset Shortcut Investigation

  Detailed distribution auditing revealed multiple direct, deterministic shortcut signals present in Testbed A:

  #### 1. Destination Port (`resp_p`)
  - **Malicious C2**: Exactly **100.00%** (12,038 of 12,038 flows) connect to port `8443`.
  - **Benign**: Port `8443` is contacted **0 times**. Benign flows connect to port 443 (70,269 flows), port 0 (11,017 flows), and port 123 (2,595 flows).
  - **Audit Test**: A decision tree trained on only `resp_p` achieves **100.00% Accuracy, 100.00% Balanced Accuracy, and 0.00% FPR** across all 4 holdout folds.
  - **Significance**: Any model with access to `resp_p` can achieve 100% test accuracy by acting as a single-rule port firewall, without learning anything about command-and-control behavior.

  #### 2. TLS Fingerprinting (`ja4` / `ja4s`)
  - **Malicious C2**: **99.94%** (12,031 of 12,038 flows) have the exact client fingerprint:
    `t13i131000_f57a46bbacb6_e5728521abd4`
    (The remaining 7 flows have missing/unnegotiated TLS handshakes).
  - **Benign**: This JA4 hash appears **0 times** in the 83,881 benign flows.
  - **Audit Test**: A model trained solely on `ja4` achieves **99.99% Accuracy and 99.97% Balanced Accuracy**, with only 7 false negatives (the 7 missing handshakes) and 0 false positives.
  - **Significance**: In real networks, JA4 client hashes represent client software implementations. In this testbed, JA4 simply fingerprints the specific Go TLS client used by the Sliver implant container.

  #### 3. Connection State (`conn_state`)
  - **Malicious C2**: **94.26%** (11,347 of 12,038 flows) end in `RSTO` (connection reset by the originator/implant). Only 5.74% end in `SF`.
  - **Benign**: Only **1.56%** of benign flows end in `RSTO`. 85.21% end in `SF`, and 13.13% end in `OTH`.
  - **Audit Test**: A single-feature `conn_state` model achieves **99.11% Accuracy and 97.14% Balanced Accuracy**.
  - **Significance**: The implant process abruptly tears down its TCP sockets after beacon exchanges, creating a persistent connection termination artifact.

  #### 4. Host IP Leakage (`id.resp_h`, `id.orig_h`)
  - **Destination IP**: 100% of malicious C2 flows connect to internal gateway IP `172.20.0.10`. Exactly 0 benign flows connect to this address (benign flows contact 113 distinct external public web endpoints).
  - **Source IP**: 100% of malicious flows originate from `172.20.0.5`.
  - **Significance**: While IP addresses were excluded from Phase 1 training features, any model trained in an environment where endpoints are not randomized is exposed to correlated topological artifacts.

  ### 2.4 Why Does the Pure Flow Statistics Model Still Score 100% on Testbed A?
  Notice that in Experiment **TQH-5**, even when all identifiers, ports, TLS hashes, protocols, and connection states are completely stripped away—leaving only 8 generic packet/byte flow statistics—the model still achieves **100.00% accuracy across unseen capture folds**.

  Is this evidence of true generalized C2 detection? **No.**

  Analysis of the raw flow volume statistics reveals an intra-testbed collection artifact:
  - In Testbed A, every malicious flow was generated by the same automated Sliver implant executing periodic polling tasks inside a container without real user interaction.
  - The resulting TLS exchanges produced deterministic packet sequences:
    - 87.2% of C2 flows had exactly 11 to 14 client packets (`orig_pkts`) and 10 to 13 server packets (`resp_pkts`).
    - Total client bytes (`orig_bytes`) and server bytes (`resp_bytes`) followed an extremely tight bimodal distribution corresponding to empty beacon polls vs tasking checks.
    - Meanwhile, the benign traffic in Testbed A was collected from web browsers loading public websites with highly variable multi-megabyte transfers, streaming media, and HTTP keep-alives.

  Thus, within the confines of Testbed A, the classifier learned the exact byte/packet envelope of that specific containerized Sliver deployment.

  ## 3. Cross-Testbed Generalization Collapse

  To conclusively test whether the pure flow-statistics model learned generalized C2 behavior or merely memorized Testbed A's artifactual flow profile, we subjected it to **out-of-distribution transfer evaluation**.

  We trained the 8-feature pure flow statistics model on the entirety of Testbed A (Sliver / TLS) and evaluated it directly, without fine-tuning, on:
  1. **Testbed B**: Merlin C2 communicating over **QUIC (UDP/443)**.
  2. **Testbed C**: Mythic C2 communicating over **HTTP with AES encryption**.

  Neither port nor TLS features were included, ensuring a semantically valid common feature space across all three testbeds:
  `['duration', 'orig_bytes', 'resp_bytes', 'orig_pkts', 'resp_pkts', 'orig_ip_bytes', 'resp_ip_bytes', 'missed_bytes']`.

  ### 3.3 Root Cause of Generalization Collapse
  1. **Transport Layer Protocol Shifts**: QUIC operates over UDP with connection multiplexing, altered header overheads, and combined handshake/data frames. A model that learned the byte and packet boundaries of TCP-based TLS completely misinterprets QUIC flow counters.
  2. **C2 Protocol & Payload Shifts**: Mythic uses HTTP POST transactions with custom base64/JSON encoding, whereas Sliver uses raw TLS byte streams. Packet counts and byte ratios differ by orders of magnitude.
  3. **Takeaway**: Flow-level volume metrics (bytes/packets/duration) are not invariant signatures of C2 behavior. They are tightly coupled to the specific protocol implementation, agent configuration, and network stack.

  ## 4. CICIDS-2018 Deep Audit: Duplication, Temporal Bursting, and Shortcuts

  ### 4.1 Dataset Profile & Record Duplication
  The audited dataset (`data/cicids2018/processed/friday_botnet.parquet`) comprises 99,538 network flows extracted from the official CICIDS-2018 Friday-02-03-2018 collection:
  - **Benign**: 19,818 flows (19.91%)
  - **Bot**: 79,720 flows (80.09%)

  #### Audit of Duplicate Flow Records
  A systematic hash comparison of all 68 feature vectors revealed severe duplication:
  - **Total Rows**: 99,538
  - **Unique Feature Vectors**: 60,716
  - **Duplicate Rows**: **38,822 (39.00% of the entire dataset)**
  - **Duplicates by Class**:
    - Benign class duplicates: 132 flows (0.67% of benign)
    - Bot class duplicates: **38,690 flows (48.53% of all bot flows are duplicates)**

  #### Split Contamination in Random 80/20 Splits
  When evaluating with a standard random train/test split (as used in Phase 1 and widely in cybersecurity literature):
  - **Test Set Size**: 19,908 rows
  - **Contaminated Test Rows**: **7,859 test flows (39.48%)** have an exact identical feature twin in the training set.
  - **Verdict**: In a random split, nearly 40% of the test set is tested on records the model has already memorized during training.

  ---

  ### 4.2 Destination Port Shortcut (`Dst Port`)
  Analysis of destination ports in CICIDS-2018 revealed a near-perfect shortcut:
  - **Bot Class**: **78,597 of 79,720 flows (98.59%)** target destination port `8080`.
  - **Benign Class**: Exactly **0 flows** target port `8080` (benign flows target ports 80, 443, 53, 21, etc.).
  - **Audit Test**: A single-feature decision tree using only `Dst Port` achieves:
    - **Accuracy**: 99.84%
    - **Balanced Accuracy**: 99.83%
    - **Macro F1**: 0.9975
    - **ROC-AUC**: 0.9996
    - **False Positives**: 7 (out of 3,964 benign test flows)
    - **False Negatives**: 25 (out of 15,944 bot test flows)

  ---

  ### 4.3 Temporal Burst Confounding
  Analysis of flow timestamps revealed extreme temporal clustering:
  - **100% of Bot flows** were generated in two discrete operational bursts:
    - Burst 1: `01:00:00 – 03:00:00` (Ares botnet execution)
    - Burst 2: `10:00:00 – 12:00:00`
  - **Zero bot flows** were recorded during any other hours of the capture.
  - **Benign traffic** was recorded continuously across the day.
  - **Implication**: Any feature correlated with background diurnal network load (such as inter-arrival times or flow rates) inadvertently acts as an indicator of attack execution time rather than attack behavior.

  ## 6. Methodological & Preprocessing Audit

  ### Preprocessing Fit Boundary Verification
  We reviewed the pipeline implementations (`xshield/pipeline.py`, `train_tqh_c2.py`, and `train_cicids2018.py`).
  - **Target Leakage**: No target label (`label` or `Label`) was inadvertently included in the feature matrices.
  - **Identifier Leakage**: Host IPs (`id.orig_h`, `id.resp_h`), flow IDs (`uid`), and timestamps were excluded from model training in the baseline pipelines.
  - **Preprocessing Fits**: In Phase 1 training scripts, scalers and one-hot encoders were fit inside `ColumnTransformer` pipelines attached to estimators. In our audit scripts, we verified that every `fit()` call occurred strictly on training partitions:
    ```python
    # Verified Audit Pattern:
    preprocessor.fit(X_train)
    X_train_trans = preprocessor.transform(X_train)
    X_test_trans  = preprocessor.transform(X_test)
    ```
  - **Finding**: The inflated metrics are **not** caused by software bugs or improper preprocessing bounds. They are caused by **structural properties of the underlying datasets themselves** (shortcuts, homogeneous generation, duplicate flows, and transport-specific signatures).

  ## 7. Limitations: Benchmark Performance vs. Real-World Network Detection

  The findings of this audit demonstrate why benchmark performance in academic intrusion detection datasets rarely translates to operational success in production Security Operations Centers (SOC) or Network Detection and Response (NDR) tools.

  ```
  +-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
  |               THE REALITY GAP IN NETWORK INTRUSION DETECTION                      |
  +-------------------------------------------------------+---------------------------+
  | Synthetic Benchmark Testbeds                          | Real-World Enterprise Net |
  +-------------------------------------------------------+---------------------------+
  | - Static, fixed destination ports (8443, 8080)        | - Port agility & hopping  |
  | - Single C2 framework (Sliver only)                   | - Diverse implants/RATs   |
  | - Homogeneous TLS client stacks (single JA4 hash)     | - Malleable TLS & JA4+    |
  | - Fixed beacon cadences and payload lengths           | - High jitter & sleep     |
  | - Clean, isolated virtual sandboxes                   | - Asymmetric routing      |
  | - High attack ratio (12% - 80% attack flows)          | - Extreme rarity (1 in 1M)|
  +-------------------------------------------------------+---------------------------+
  ```

  ### 1. Port Agility and Domain Fronting
  In real-world red team engagements and advanced persistent threat (APT) campaigns, operators do not bind C2 traffic to obvious non-standard static ports like `8443` or `8080` without legitimate cover. They utilize:
  - Standard port 443 with valid TLS certificates matching legitimate domains.
  - Domain fronting, CDN proxies (Cloudflare, Fastly, CloudFront), and redirectors.
  - Port agility and protocol tunneling (DNS over HTTPS, WebSocket tunnels).
  A classifier that relies on port numbers is trivially bypassed by changing a single configuration line in the C2 profile.

  ### 2. Malleable C2 Profiles and Client Diversity
  Modern C2 frameworks (Cobalt Strike Malleable C2, Mythic, Sliver, Havoc, Brute Ratel) allow operators to customize every aspect of the network signature:
  - HTTP headers, URIs, and user-agents can be matched to Microsoft Edge, Google Chrome, or Windows Update.
  - TLS ClientHellos can be shaped using tools like `JA3/JA4` mutators or custom TLS fingerprint impersonation.
  - The single-hash JA4 shortcut discovered in Testbed A (`t13i131000...`) only exists because the implant was deployed with default Go `crypto/tls` library parameters. Changing the TLS library or running over native OS sockets invalidates the fingerprint immediately.

  ### 3. Flow Volume Invariance Breakdown
  As proven by our cross-testbed experiments (Testbed A to B and C), flow-level statistics (bytes per flow, packets per flow, flow duration) are **not transport-invariant**:
  - Moving from TLS to QUIC alters packet framing, acknowledges multiple packets per frame, and combines connection establishment with cryptographic exchange.
  - Beacon jitter, packet bundling, and varying command outputs constantly change byte sizes.
  - Training an ML model on raw flow byte counts produces a classifier that detects a specific software version, not malicious intent.

  ### 4. The Operational Base-Rate Fallacy
  In an enterprise network processing 500 million network flows per day:
  - A false positive rate of **0.01%** (which would look outstanding on an academic benchmark) yields **50,000 false positive alerts every single day**.
  - In our cross-testbed evaluations, the false positive rates were **1.57%** (Testbed B) and **15.89%** (Testbed C). At that rate, security analysts would be inundated with millions of false alarms, completely destroying operational utility.
  - Benchmark evaluations where attacks represent 12% to 80% of the dataset create a completely false sense of precision and false alarm tolerance.

  ## 8. Concrete Recommendations for Future Development

  1. **Mandatory Exclusion of Identity & Shortcut Features**:
    - Explicitly ban destination port (`resp_p`, `Dst Port`), IP addresses, and static TLS fingerprints from generalized behavioral classifiers.
    - Maintain ports and fingerprints in a deterministic **Threat Intelligence / IOC matching tier**, completely separated from the machine learning inference tier.

  2. **Transition from Static Flow Stats to Behavioral Sequence Metrics**:
    - Replace point-in-time byte/packet totals with multi-flow temporal behavior:
      - Inter-flow beacon interval regularity (coefficient of variation of inter-arrival times).
      - Beacon cadence autocorrelation across multi-hour connection graphs.
      - Ratio of beacon check-ins to payload transfers.
    - These higher-order temporal patterns persist even when ports, TLS libraries, and packet sizes change.

  3. **Multi-Testbed Joint Training & Out-of-Distribution Validation**:
    - Models must never be validated solely on the same testbed or framework on which they were trained.
    - Future validation protocols must require cross-framework testing (e.g., train on Sliver + Mythic, test on Merlin + Havoc).

  4. **De-duplication as a Standard Data Pipeline Stage**:
    - All network flow datasets must pass through strict feature-vector deduplication before splitting.
    - Evaluation splits must enforce both **temporal chronological separation** and **host/network isolation** to eliminate data leakage.
